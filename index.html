I work on responsibility, fairness, and the allocation of both — most recently on what happens to answerability when a principal deploys an autonomous artificial agent.

I read philosophy at Hong Kong Baptist University, graduating in 2021, where my early research was in social contract theory. I am currently reading part-time for a Master of Social Work at the Chinese University of Hong Kong.

My method combines conceptual analysis with formal tools drawn from cooperative game theory, and I try to carry both through to the institutional arrangements they imply. The papers below share a structure: each takes a normative position that is usually treated as indivisible — responsibility for a harm, credit for a contribution, authority over a decision — and asks how it is divided when several parties are involved and one of them may not be a person at all.

Papers

The Delegation Illusion: Why Deploying Autonomous AI Agents Does Not Diminish Principal Responsibility

Forthcoming in AI and Ethics

Abstract

I argue that the responsibility gap widely alleged for autonomous, principal-deployed AI agents is illusory for a specifiable class of cases. The inference trades on conflating a principal's causal and epistemic distance from an agent's particular acts with normative distance from the decision to deploy it: answerability, unlike attributability, does not require control over particulars, and delegation transfers execution, not standing.

Framing Fairly: A Dual Benchmark for Ethical Influence in Behavioral Design in Collective Production

Under review

Abstract

I develop a dual-principle framework for evaluating the ethics of framing and choice architecture in contexts of collective production, where several agents jointly generate value and its presentation shapes how their contributions are recognised. One principle protects agents' undiminished autonomy rights; the other, drawing on contractualism and the Shapley value, requires that a framing not misrepresent the relative size of contributions to a jointly produced surplus.

The Family Veto in Presumed-Consent Organ Donation: Procedural Standing without Substantive Override

Under review

Abstract

I argue that the family veto retained by nearly every jurisdiction that has adopted presumed consent for organ donation cannot be justified on any of the rationales offered for presumed consent itself. Families should retain a procedural role, as informants and as the addressees of institutional trust, but not a substantive power to override a registered decision.
